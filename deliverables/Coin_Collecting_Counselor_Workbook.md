### Req 1: United States Minting Process
**Official Requirement 1:** Understand how coins are made and where the active U. S. Mint facilities are located.
**Source:** *Official Scouting America Coin Collecting Merit Badge Pamphlet (p. 11, p. 15)* • **Scout Workbook Organizer (Concept & Examples Notes):** Req 1 Structured Technical Study & Explanation Matrix
- **Socratic Prompt (Ask Scouts First):** Why does the upsetting mill raise a protective rim on a planchet before striking occurs?
- **Official Pamphlet Summary & Expected Answer Key:**
  - Planchet Preparation: Annealed metal strips pass through blanking machines to punch out smooth circular discs.
  - Upsetting Mill: Raised protective rims form when planchets roll through narrow rotating groove channels.
  - Coil Press Operation: Hardened steel dies stamp obverse and reverse designs simultaneously under heavy pressure.
  - Mint Mark Identification: Distinct letters stamped on coin fields identify Philadelphia, Denver, San Francisco, or West Point.
- **Step-by-Step Facilitation & Station Procedure:**
  - Step 1 - Blanking Strip Feed: Guide rolled metal coils into high-speed blanking presses to punch out planchet discs.
  - Step 2 - Rim Milling Feed: Pass blank planchets through an upsetting machine to raise protective edge rims.
  - Step 3 - Coining Press Setup: Align paired obverse and reverse dies inside the collar of the press.
  - Step 4 - Strike Quality Review: Check newly struck coins under magnification for sharp details and centered designs.
- **Common Myth:** Scouts often believe all United States Mint facilities strike circulating coins for general retail commerce.
- **Pamphlet Fact:** Active circulating coinage facilities in Philadelphia and Denver operate high-speed presses striking blanks between steel dies.

### Req 2: Core Numismatic Terminology Standards
**Official Requirement 2:** Explain the following terms: obverse, reverse, reeding, clad, type set, and date set.
**Source:** *Official Scouting America Coin Collecting Merit Badge Pamphlet (p. 8, p. 9)* • **Scout Workbook Organizer (Concept & Examples Notes):** Req 2 Structured Technical Study & Explanation Matrix
- **Socratic Prompt (Ask Scouts First):** Why did the United States Mint introduce reeding on precious metal coinage during early federal history?
- **Official Pamphlet Summary & Expected Answer Key:**
  - Obverse Face Definition: The principal coin side features national emblems, heads of state, or Liberty.
  - Reverse Face Features: The secondary side displays statutory denominations, heraldic eagles, or commemorative artistic motifs.
  - Reeded Edge Purpose: Grooved vertical ridges prevent clipping and assist visually impaired citizens with denomination sorting.
  - Clad Layer Composition: Outer copper-nickel layers bond directly over a solid inner core of pure copper.
- **Step-by-Step Facilitation & Station Procedure:**
  - Step 1 - Orientation Examination: Rotate the coin vertically along its horizontal axis to examine obverse and reverse.
  - Step 2 - Edge Pattern Inspection: Count or inspect vertical edge grooves to confirm reeded or plain border design.
  - Step 3 - Core Band Verification: Check the outer edge layer for visible copper sandwiching on clad pieces.
  - Step 4 - Set Categorization: Sort specimens into date chronological binders or varied design representative type albums.
- **Common Myth:** Novice collectors frequently confuse type sets with complete date sets across long series runs.
- **Pamphlet Fact:** A date set gathers every calendar year of an individual denomination regardless of minor design changes.

### Req 3: Numismatic Storage System Methods
**Official Requirement 3:** Describe three different ways to store a collection, and the benefits, drawbacks, and cost of each method.
**Source:** *Official Scouting America Coin Collecting Merit Badge Pamphlet (p. 21, p. 55)* • **Scout Workbook Organizer (Concept & Examples Notes):** Req 3 Structured Technical Study & Explanation Matrix
- **Socratic Prompt (Ask Scouts First):** Why does chemical outgassing from flexible polyvinyl chloride holders destroy coin surfaces over time?
- **Official Pamphlet Summary & Expected Answer Key:**
  - Stapled mylar cardboard holders offer inexpensive archival protection for large coin accumulations.
  - Hard Plastic Capsule: Inert acrylic screw-down capsules provide superior physical impact resistance at moderate unit cost.
  - Cabinet Drawer Display: Felt-lined mahogany drawers allow safe organized viewing but require substantial initial financial investment.
  - Flexible vinyl flips leach acidic chemical softeners that leave permanent green surface residue.
- **Step-by-Step Facilitation & Station Procedure:**
  - Step 1 - Material Safety Check: Confirm coin holders are made from rigid acrylic or acid-free cardboard and mylar.
  - Step 2 - Specimen Edge Placement: Set the coin carefully onto the open interior mylar window using gloved fingers.
  - Step 3 - Fastener Staple Crimping: Staple the cardboard edges shut and flatten all wire prongs with smooth pliers.
  - Step 4 - Storage Climate Control: Store prepared holders inside a cool dry container along with fresh desiccant packs.
- **Common Myth:** Soft plastic vinyl sleeves protect coins better than stiff archival cardboard and mylar holders.
- **Pamphlet Fact:** Mylar cardboard flips provide safe, economical storage without releasing damaging polyvinyl chloride acidic softeners.

### Req 6: Federal Reserve Currency Identification
**Official Requirement 6:** Identify for your counselor the people depicted on current currency: $1, $2, $5, $10, $20, $50, and $100 notes. Explain where United States currency is printed.
**Source:** *Official Scouting America Coin Collecting Merit Badge Pamphlet (p. 25, p. 40)* • **Scout Workbook Organizer (Concept & Examples Notes):** Req 6 Structured Technical Study & Explanation Matrix
- **Socratic Prompt (Ask Scouts First):** Why does the Department of the Treasury prohibit depicting living individuals on United States currency?
- **Official Pamphlet Summary & Expected Answer Key:**
  - Gilbert Stuart portrait of George Washington anchors the face of federal currency.
