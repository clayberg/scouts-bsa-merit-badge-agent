### Req 1: Studio Safety Equipment Precautions
**Official Requirement 1:** Explain to your counselor the precautions that must be followed for the safe use and operation of a potter's tools, equipment, and other materials.
**Source:** *Official Scouting America Pottery Merit Badge Pamphlet (p. 39, p. 43)* • **Organizer (Concept & Examples Notes):** Req 1 Structured Technical Study & Explanation Matrix (Precautions That Must Be Followed)
> **Guide to Safe Scouting Warning:** Never dry sweep clay dust; always clean ceramic studios with wet mops or HEPA vacuum filtration units.
- **Common Myth:** Scouts often believe sweeping dried clay scraps with standard household brooms keeps studio air clean.
- **Pamphlet Fact:** Dry sweeping releases microscopic respirable silica particles that cause chronic irreversible lung damage.

### Req 2: Clay Properties and Potter Wheels
**Official Requirement 2 (Parent Overview):** Do the following: (a) Explain the properties and ingredients of a good clay body for the following: (1) Making sculpture using the hand-building method (2) Throwing on the wheel (b) Tell how three different kinds of potter's wheels work.
**Source:** *Official Scouting America Pottery Merit Badge Pamphlet (p. 32, p. 33)* • **Group Organizer:** Concept & Examples Notes
> **Guide to Safe Scouting Warning:** Keep feet and loose clothing clear of rotating kick wheel flywheels to prevent serious pinch injuries.
- **Common Myth:** Beginners often think identical clay formulations work equally well for throwing and large sculpted forms.
- **Pamphlet Fact:** Large sculptures need coarse grog for structural support while fine throwing clay minimizes hand abrasion.

**Official Pamphlet Summary:**
- Sculpture Body Porosity: Coarse fired grog particles open clay pores to release trapped drying moisture.
- Throwing Clay Plasticity: Fine platelet grain sizes provide optimal surface tension for pulling tall cylinders.
- Kick Wheel Mechanics: Heavy concrete flywheels store mechanical momentum through repeated manual foot kicking motions.
- Electric Wheel Controls: Variable speed direct drive motors deliver continuous torque under heavy centering pressures.

**Official Requirement 4:** Explain the meaning of the following pottery terms: bat, wedging, throwing, leather hard, bone dry, greenware, bisque, terra-cotta, grog, slip, score, earthenware, stoneware, porcelain, pyrometric cone, and glaze.
**Source:** *Official Scouting America Pottery Merit Badge Pamphlet (p. 79, p. 82)* • **Organizer (Concept & Examples Notes):** Req 4 Structured Technical Study & Explanation Matrix (Pottery Terms Bat Wedging Throwing)

### Req 5: Ceramic Fabrication and Kiln Firing
**Official Requirement 5 (Parent Overview):** Do the following: Each piece is to be painted, glazed, or otherwise decorated by you. (a) Make a slab pot, a coil pot, and a pinch pot. (b) Make a human or animal figurine or decorative sculpture. (c) Throw a functional form on a potter's wheel. (d) Help an experienced adult to load and fire a kiln OR describe in detail how to load and fire a kiln.
**Source:** *Official Scouting America Pottery Merit Badge Pamphlet (p. 13, p. 64)* • **Group Organizer:** Skill Demonstration Checklist
> **Guide to Safe Scouting Warning:** Ensure all pottery is bone dry before firing to prevent explosive steam ruptures inside the kiln.
- **Common Myth:** Novices often assume air bubbles trapped in clay expand and blow up pieces inside kilns.
- **Pamphlet Fact:** Explosions occur when rapidly boiling trapped water converts into steam before escaping clay pores.

**Official Pamphlet Summary:**
- Rolling uniform slabs prevents localized drying stress that warps flat structural plates.
- Pinch Pot Compression: Gentle rhythmic thumb pressure compresses clay walls from base upward toward rim.
- Wheel Centering Force: Anchoring elbows against thighs stabilizes hands to push wobble out of spinning clay.
- Painting alumina kiln wash on shelves prevents melted glazes from bonding permanently.

### Req 7: Kiln Exploration and Cultural History
**Official Requirement 7 (Parent Overview):** With your parent or guardian's permission and your counselor's approval, do ONE of the following: (a) Visit the kiln yard at a local college or other craft school. Learn how the different kinds of kilns work, including low fire electric, gas or propane high fire, wood or salt/soda, and raku. (b) Visit a museum, art exhibit, art gallery, artists' co-op, or artist's studio that features pottery. After your visit, share with your counselor what you have learned. (c) Using resources from the library, magazines, the internet (with your parent or guardian's permission), and other outlets, learn about the historical and cultural importance of pottery. Share what you discovered with your counselor.
**Source:** *Official Scouting America Pottery Merit Badge Pamphlet (p. 25, p. 84)* • **Group Organizer:** Concept & Examples Notes
> **Guide to Safe Scouting Warning:** Stay behind marked safety boundaries around active kiln yards to avoid severe thermal contact burns.
> **Starting Gate (Counselor Pre-Approval Required):** Counselor Pre-Approval Required - Review your Pottery Req 7 preparation with your Merit Badge Counselor before moving to subsequent tasks.
- **Common Myth:** Visitors assume all kilns produce identical glaze surfaces regardless of fuel source used.
- **Pamphlet Fact:** Combustion fuels like gas and wood alter kiln atmospheres, creating unique reduction color variations.

**Official Pamphlet Summary:**
- Oxidation Firing Atmosphere: Electric kilns supply abundant atmospheric oxygen to produce clean, bright glaze coloration.
- Reduction Fuel Firing: Gas kilns starve chambers of oxygen, pulling oxides from clay bodies directly.
- Common salt introduced into hot kilns vaporizes to produce orange-peel sodium surfaces.
- Indigenous Vessel Heritage: Native American coil vessels utilized local earthenware clays burnished with river stones.
