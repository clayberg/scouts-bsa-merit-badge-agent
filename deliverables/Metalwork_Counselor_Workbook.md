### Req 3: Work-Hardening, Annealing, and Tempering
**Official Requirement 3 (Parent Overview):** Do the following: (a) Work-harden a piece of 26- or 28-gauge sheet brass or sheet copper. Put a 45-degree bend in the metal, then heavily peen the area along the bend line to work-harden it. Note the amount of effort that is required to overcome the yield point in this unworked piece of metal. (b) Soften the work-hardened piece from requirement 3(a) by annealing it, and then try to remove the 45-degree bend. Note the amount of effort that is required to overcome the yield point. (c) Make a temper color index from a flat piece of steel. Using hand tools, make and temper a center punch of medium-carbon or high-carbon steel.
**Source:** *Official Scouting America Metalwork Merit Badge Pamphlet (p. 22, p. 37)* • **Group Organizer:** Skill Demonstration Checklist
> **Guide to Safe Scouting Warning:** Review task hazards with your counselor before starting Metalwork Requirement 3 on work-hardening, annealing, and tempering: inspect tools and work surfaces before use, keep bystanders clear of active demonstrations, and follow BSA Guide to Safe Scouting supervision standards.
- **Common Myth:** Hammering along a bend in sheet copper or brass stretches and weakens the metal so that it bends back flat with less effort than before.
- **Pamphlet Fact:** Heavily peening a bend in 26- or 28-gauge brass or copper compacts and distorts its internal crystal grain structure (work-hardening), raising its yield point so the bend resists straightening until it is annealed with heat (p. 22).

**Official Pamphlet Summary:**
- Elastic Springback in Cold Metal: Cold metal does not always stay in place after it is bent (p. 22).
- Bending Past the Yield Point: To overcome thespringback effect, the metal often must be bent past its yield point (p. 22).
- Hand-Bending Unworked Sheet Metal: Without using any tools, do your best to put a 45-degree bend in the unworked metal (p. 22).
- Demonstrating Springback at Forty-Five Degrees: Stop when you have pushed the metal to To demonstrate spring back, use your 45 degrees (p. 22).

**Official Requirement 3a:** Work-harden a piece of 26- or 28-gauge sheet brass or sheet copper. Put a 45-degree bend in the metal, then heavily peen the area along the bend line to work-harden it. - **Pamphlet Fact:** All common metals except lead and mercury work-harden when hammered at room temperature because lead recrystallizes below room temperature and mercury is liquid (p. 22).

**Source:** *Official Scouting America Metalwork Merit Badge Pamphlet (p. 87, p. 88)* • **Group Organizer:** Career & Profession Exploration Card
> **Guide to Safe Scouting Warning:** BSA Youth Protection & Buddy System (Req 4): Complete all Metalworking Careers and Hobbies discussions and demonstrations with Two-Deep Leadership and your patrol buddy.
- **Common Myth:** Every modern metalworking career requires a four-year university engineering degree, or conversely, offers no path for college-educated engineers.
- **Pamphlet Fact:** Metalworking careers span both skilled trades entered through vocational schools and apprenticeships (machinists, sheet metal mechanics, welders, tool and die makers) and university-degreed metallurgical, structural, and mechanical engineers (p. 87).

**Official Pamphlet Summary:**
- Vocational Training and Apprenticeships: Many metal workers need a high school education, followed by vocational school or an apprenticeship, to become professional hands-on metal workers (p. 87).
- Shipyard, Auto, and Aircraft Jobs: Some may choose to work for a shipyard, an automobile-parts manufacturing plant, or an aircraft manufacturing plant (p. 87).
- Specialty Machine Shop Careers: Others may decide to work for large machine shops that specialize in rebuilding engines or manufacturing specialty parts for other companies (p. 87).
- Engineering Degrees in Metal Fields: Some professionals with interests in metal working earn college degrees in mechanical, structural, or metallurgical engineering (p. 87).
