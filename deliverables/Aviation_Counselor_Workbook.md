## 2. Pre-Class Prerequisite Checklist ('Bring Proof to Class')

- [ ] **Req 3 (Explain):** Flight Operations. Do TWO of the following: (a) Using a flight simulator software package, set a course and fly the headings you have established with a successful take-off and landing. (b) Under supervision, perform a preflight inspection of an aircraft. (c) Observe and/or participate in an aircraft maintenance activity. Describe the maintenance schedule and requirements for an aircraft of your choice. (d) Obtain and learn how to read an aeronautical chart. Measure a true course on the chart; correct it for magnetic variation, compass deviation, and wind drift to determine a navigational heading for an aircraft. - [ ] **Req 4 (Prepare):** Airport Operations. Do ONE of the following: (a) Visit an airport. After the visit, report on how the facilities are used, how runways are numbered, and how runways are determined to be "active." (b) Visit a Federal Aviation Administration facility: Airport Traffic Control Tower (ATCT), Terminal Radar Approach Control (TRACON), Air Route Traffic Control Center (ARTCC), or Flight Standards District Office (FSDO). Report on the operation and your impressions of the facility. (c) Visit a military aviation facility. Learn how that facility supports defense and/or civilian activities. Report on the operation and your impressions of the facility. (d) Visit an aviation museum or attend an air show. Report on your impressions of the museum or show, and what you learned from the experience.

### Req 1: Aviation Basics and Flight Mechanics
**Official Requirement 1 (Parent Overview):** Aviation Basics and Mechanics of Flight. Do the following: (a) Define "aircraft." Describe three kinds of aircraft today, and their typical uses. (b) Provide a brief overview of the evolution of flight, and discuss three notable times in history important to aviation. (c) Explain the difference between a fixed wing and a rotary wing aircraft, and the benefits of each. (d) Explain the operation of piston, turbine, and jet engines. (e) Using a model aircraft, describe the four forces that act on an aircraft in flight. (f) Explain how an airfoil generates lift, specifically noting Bernoulli's principle. (g) Identify and describe the aerodynamic control surfaces on the aircraft of your choice, and explain how they operate to control its attitude and direction of flight. (h) Explain the purposes and functions of the various instruments found in a typical single-engine aircraft: attitude indicator, heading indicator, altimeter, airspeed indicator, turn and bank indicator, vertical speed indicator, compass, navigation, communication, and engine performance indicators.
**Source:** *Official Scouting America Aviation Merit Badge Pamphlet (p. 28, p. 34)* • **Group Organizer:** 2-Column Comparison Table
> **Guide to Safe Scouting Warning:** Review task hazards with your counselor before starting Aviation Requirement 1 on aviation basics and flight mechanics: inspect tools and work surfaces before use, keep bystanders clear of active demonstrations, and follow BSA Guide to Safe Scouting supervision standards.
- **Common Myth:** An airplane wing generates lift only when the nose is pointed upward to push against the oncoming wind.
- **Pamphlet Fact:** The curved upper surface of an airfoil speeds up passing air and lowers air pressure above the wing according to Bernoulli's principle, creating lift even in level flight (p. 28).

**Official Pamphlet Summary:**
- The last three instruments, tachometer, oil pressure gauge, and oil temperature gauge, tell a pilot how the engine is operating (p. 28).
- The attitude indicator or "artificial horizon" lets the pilot get an immediate picture of the airplane's attitude, which is its position relative to Earth's (p. 28).
- Attached to a gyroscope is a face with acontrasting horizon line (p. 28).
- Tracking Pitch and Roll: This representation of the horizon moves to show the plane's position with respect to the real horizon (p. 28).

**Official Requirement 3 (Parent Overview):** Flight Operations. Do TWO of the following: (a) Using a flight simulator software package, set a course and fly the headings you have established with a successful take-off and landing. (b) Under supervision, perform a preflight inspection of an aircraft. (c) Observe and/or participate in an aircraft maintenance activity. Describe the maintenance schedule and requirements for an aircraft of your choice. (d) Obtain and learn how to read an aeronautical chart. Measure a true course on the chart; correct it for magnetic variation, compass deviation, and wind drift to determine a navigational heading for an aircraft. (e) With your parent or guardian's permission, take a discovery flight in an aircraft. Record the date, place, type of aircraft, and duration of flight. Report on your impressions of the flight.
**Source:** *Official Scouting America Aviation Merit Badge Pamphlet (p. 50, p. 54)* • **Group Organizer:** Activity & Observation Log
> **Guide to Safe Scouting Warning:** BSA Youth Protection & Buddy System (Req 3): Complete all Flight Operations and Navigation discussions and demonstrations with Two-Deep Leadership and your patrol buddy.
> **Starting Gate (Parent / Guardian Permission Required):** Parent/Guardian Consent & Review - Review and complete Aviation Req 3 with your parent or guardian's approval in compliance with BSA Youth Protection.
- **Common Myth:** Magnetic variation and compass deviation are two names for the exact same geographic difference between true north and magnetic north.
- **Pamphlet Fact:** Magnetic variation is the angle between true north and magnetic north on a chart, whereas compass deviation is caused by magnetic interference from electronics and metal inside the airplane (p. 50).

**Official Pamphlet Summary:**
- Calculating Aircraft Compass Deviation: Technicians can calculate closely just how much the deviation will be for any airplane (p. 50).
- The Cockpit Deviation Card: In every aircraft, you will find a deviation card mounted near the compass that tells us how to correct the magnetic course for deviation (p. 50).
- Remember, the magnetic course from Indianapolis International (IND) to Crawfordsville Airport (CFJ) is 300 degrees (p. 50).
- Applying the Compass Correction: Our deviation card tells us that for a magnetic course of 300 degrees, we should steer a course of 301 degrees (p. 50).

- **Common Myth:** Runway 27 is the twenty-seventh runway built at a large international airport.
- **Pamphlet Fact:** Runways are numbered by rounding their magnetic compass heading to the nearest ten degrees and dropping the last zero, so Runway 27 points west at 270 degrees (p. 73).

- Flight Service Station Role: Providing weather and information services for all pilots is the primary responsibility of the flight service station (p. 73).
- As its name suggests, a control tower is an elevated structure from which airport controllers operate (p. 73).
- The people in the control tower have four primary responsibilities (p. 73).
- The actual TRACON facility might not be located in the control tower or even on the airport site (p. 73).

- **Common Myth:** A pilot with a Private Pilot certificate can legally be hired to fly passengers or cargo for compensation.
- **Pamphlet Fact:** Private pilots may carry passengers and share certain operating expenses, but flying for compensation or hire requires at least a Commercial Pilot certificate (p. 58).

**Official Pamphlet Summary:**
- The recreational pilot certificate has some important restrictions (p. 57).
- Daylight and Airspace Rules: Most importantly, the pilot can fly only during daylight hours and in airspace where he or she does not need to communicate with airtraffic control (p. 57).
- Accessible Entry-Level Certificate: Of all the certificates and ratings, the recreational pilot certificate is the easiest to obtain (p. 57).
- Thirty-Hour Training Requirement: It requires 30 hours of flight instruction (three hours of which must be solo flight time) (p. 57).
