## 2. Pre-Class Prerequisite Checklist ('Bring Proof to Class')

- [ ] **Req 9 (Prepare):** Taking Action. Do ONE of the following: (a) Develop a fire readiness plan for a wildland area with which you are familiar. The plan should include a map showing available resources, water supplies, natural and manmade barriers, and access. The plan should discuss fuel loads, available fire apparatus, structure protection needs, values at risk, medical and evacuation considerations, and potential fire suppression tactics. (b) Visit with a state or federal forestry official or your local fire warden. Discuss the causes of and types of wildland fire that occur in your area. Discuss the prime fire season(s) in your area. Identify the agency responsible for wildland fire suppression in your area. Discuss what you learned with your counselor. (c) Conduct a Firewise assessment of a home or a building in the wildland-urban interface, which could include a Scout camp building or facility. Identify potential risks and fire hazards. With your counselor's approval, complete a project to reduce the wildfire risk and increase the preparedness of the building. Write a brief report about what you did and learned from the assessment and project. (d) Meet with a Fire Prevention Specialist or a local fire official to learn about fire prevention efforts in your community. From what you learned, prepare a fire prevention message (skit, video, or billboard concept) and share with your counselor and troop (or other group approved by your counselor). (e) Meet with a meteorologist or someone familiar with fire weather and discuss the fire weather concerns and typical fire seasons that exist in your area. Discuss the conditions that lead to a Fire Weather Watch or a Red Flag Warning. Discuss what you learned with your counselor.
- [ ] **Req 11 (Prepare):** Careers in Wildland Fire Management. Explore careers related to this merit badge. Research one career to learn about the training and education needed, costs, job prospects, salary, job duties, and career advancement. Your research methods may include, with your parent or guardian's permission, an internet or library search, an interview with a professional in the field, or a visit to a location where people in this career work. Discuss with your counselor both your findings and what about this profession might make it an interesting career.

### Req 3: Prescribed Fire Resource Management
**Official Requirement 3 (Parent Overview):** Fire as a Management Tool. Discuss with your counselor how prescribed fire is used to accomplish the following: (a) Fuel reduction (b) Fish and wildlife habitat enhancement (c) Ecosystem restoration (d) Forest regeneration (e) Insect and disease control
**Source:** *Official Scouting America Wildland Fire Management Merit Badge Pamphlet (p. 8, p. 9)* • **Group Organizer:** Concept & Examples Notes
- **Common Myth:** Prescribed fire always burns out of control and permanently damages the surrounding forest ecosystem.
- **Pamphlet Fact:** Prescribed burning applies low-intensity fire under precise weather parameters to reduce fuels, improve wildlife browse, and regenerate fire-adapted tree species.

**Official Pamphlet Summary:**
- Certified burn plans establish strict humidity, wind, and fuel moisture prescription boundaries.
- Surface Fuel Reduction: Consuming leaf litter and dead wood prevents future catastrophic high-intensity crown fires.
- Nutrient Mineralization: Low-intensity fire mineralizes organic matter, releasing calcium and nitrogen into native forest soil.
- Seed Stratification: Serotinous cones open and drop viable seeds only when heated by fire.

(b) Wildfire Mitigation: (1) Wildland-Urban Interface (2) Defensible space and how homes and communities in the wildland-urban interface can be protected from wildfire
**Source:** *Official Scouting America Wildland Fire Management Merit Badge Pamphlet (p. 6, p. 8)* • **Group Organizer:** Concept & Examples Notes
- **Common Myth:** Most catastrophic wildfires are started by lightning strikes rather than preventable human negligence.
- **Pamphlet Fact:** Over eighty percent of wildland fires in the United States result from human causes, including unattended campfires, debris burning, and vehicle sparks.

**Official Pamphlet Summary:**
- Human Ignitions: Careless debris burning and abandoned campfires cause the vast majority of wildfires.
- Appliance Safety: Spark arrestors on chainsaws and lawnmowers prevent dangerous roadside and field ignitions.
- Homeowners create defensible space zones to protect structures from intense radiant heat.
- Ember Intrusion: Fine mesh vent screens block flying embers from entering home attics and eaves.

### Req 5: Fire Behavior and Environment
**Official Requirement 5 (Parent Overview):** Fire Behavior. Do the following: (a) Discuss with your counselor how the fire environment affects wildland fire behavior. Include examples of the influences of weather, topography, and fuel. (b) Explain how a wildfire can be suppressed by removing heat, fuel, or oxygen. (c) Draw a diagram to illustrate the parts of a wildfire.
**Source:** *Official Scouting America Wildland Fire Management Merit Badge Pamphlet (p. 6, p. 8)* • **Group Organizer:** Field Sketch & Diagram Canvas
- **Common Myth:** Wildfires always spread uphill because the flames are blown upward by gentle valley breezes alone.
- **Pamphlet Fact:** Fire behavior is dictated by the wildland fire environment, which consists of fuel, weather, and topography.

**Official Pamphlet Summary:**
- Fire Triangle: Wildland combustion requires oxygen, ambient heat, and flammable wildland vegetative fuel.
- Topographic Preheating: Rising convection currents preheat uphill vegetation, rapidly accelerating uphill fire spread rates.
- Fuel Moisture: Fine dead fuels respond rapidly to hourly changes in ambient relative humidity.
- Wildfire Anatomy: A wildland fire consists of head, flanks, rear, fingers, and spot fires.

### Req 6: Wildland Firefighter Personal Safety
**Official Requirement 6 (Parent Overview):** Wildland Firefighter Safety. Do the following: (a) Discuss with your counselor the personal protective equipment used by wildland firefighters and its use. (b) Discuss the following with your counselor, including why each is an important consideration for maintaining personal safety and situational awareness during wildland fire suppression activities: (1) 10 Standard Firefighting Orders (2) 18 Watch Out Situations (3) Lookouts, Communication, Escape Routes, and Safety Zones (LCES)
**Source:** *Official Scouting America Wildland Fire Management Merit Badge Pamphlet (p. 7, p. 8)* • **Group Organizer:** Concept & Examples Notes
> **Guide to Safe Scouting Warning:** Never wear synthetic polyester or nylon garments on a wildland fire incident because synthetics melt into skin under radiant heat.
- **Common Myth:** Structural turnout coats and heavy air tanks provide ideal protection for mobile wildland fire crews.
- **Pamphlet Fact:** Wildland firefighters wear flame-resistant Nomex clothing, eight-inch leather boots, hard hats, eye protection, and leather gloves, and carry emergency fire shelters.

**Official Pamphlet Summary:**
- Nomex Fabric: Aramid clothing resists direct flame and high heat without melting onto skin.
- Fire Shelter: Aluminum foil shelters reflect radiant heat when trapped by sudden fire entrapment.
- Post dedicated lookouts who maintain continuous visual contact with the fire.
- Maintain clear unblocked escape routes leading directly to predetermined safety zones.
