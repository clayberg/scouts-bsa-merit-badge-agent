### Req 1: Composite Fabrication Hazard Management
**Official Requirement 1 (Parent Overview):** Do the following: (a) Explain to your counselor the most likely hazards you may encounter while working with composite materials and what you should do to anticipate, mitigate, prevent, and respond to these hazards. Describe the appropriate safety gear and clothing that should be used when working with composite materials. (b) Explain the precautions that must be taken when handling, storing, and disposing of resins, reinforcements, and other materials used in composites. Include in your discussion the importance of health, safety, and environmental responsibility and awareness. (c) Describe what a safety data sheet (SDS) is and tell why it is used.
**Source:** *Official Scouting America Composite Materials Merit Badge Pamphlet (p. 44, p. 46)* • **Group Organizer:** Concept & Examples Notes
> **Guide to Safe Scouting Warning:** Work exclusively in cross-ventilated spaces with an operational eyewash station and an ABC dry chemical extinguisher.
- **Common Myth:** Resin mixtures cure strictly through room air drying rather than an internal exothermic chemical reaction.
- **Pamphlet Fact:** Resin crosslinking generates exothermic heat that can melt mixing cups and ignite surrounding shop trash.

**Official Pamphlet Summary:**
- Vapor Control: Active exhaust ventilation removes volatile organic styrene fumes from indoor laminating work areas.
- Resin Toxicity: Uncured epoxy and polyester liquid resins irritate human skin and trigger severe sensitization.
- Fiber Protection: Airborne glass particulates penetrate lung tissue without tightly fitted particulate respirators.
- Thermal Hazard: Catalyzed thermoset liquid generates rapid exothermic heat that can melt ordinary mixing vessels.

- **Common Myth:** Composite materials were first invented during the space program of the nineteen sixties.
- **Pamphlet Fact:** Modern carbon fiber composites provide higher specific tensile strength per pound than heat-treated alloy steel.

**Official Pamphlet Summary:**
- Ancient Composites: Straw mixed with mud bricks created durable structural building units in ancient Mesopotamia.

- Material Anisotropy: Fiber composites carry directional loads efficiently unlike isotropic metals with uniform structural strength.
- Corrosion Resistance: Fiberglass structures resist marine galvanic degradation far better than structural carbon steel plates.

- **Common Myth:** All composite reinforcement fabrics possess identical tensile strength, stiffness, and electrical conductivity profiles.
- **Pamphlet Fact:** Aramid fibers absorb kinetic energy exceptionally well but degrade rapidly when exposed to ultraviolet radiation.

- Matrix Systems: Polyester, vinylester, and epoxy resins bind fibers and determine ultimate thermal endurance.

Discuss what you learned with your counselor. (b) Find three composites-related websites. Share and discuss what you learned with your counselor.
**Source:** *Official Scouting America Composite Materials Merit Badge Pamphlet (p. 40, p. 41)* • **Group Organizer:** Concept & Examples Notes
> **Guide to Safe Scouting Warning:** Wear certified steel-toe work boots, safety glasses, and hearing protection during manufacturing plant walkthroughs.
> **Starting Gate (Counselor Pre-Approval Required):** Counselor Pre-Approval Required - Review your Composite Materials Req 4 preparation with your Merit Badge Counselor before moving to subsequent tasks.
- **Common Myth:** Composite components are exclusively manufactured by hand brushing liquid resin into open female molds.
- **Pamphlet Fact:** Industrial vacuum resin infusion processes pull liquid polymers through dry reinforcement stacks using atmospheric pressure.

**Official Pamphlet Summary:**
- Autoclave Curing: Pressurized heated autoclaves eliminate structural voids and consolidate aerospace composite prepreg plies.
- Vacuum Infusion: Atmospheric pressure drives liquid resin through sealed dry fibers under airtight nylon films.
- Pultrusion Speed: Continuous pulling of resin-wetted roving strands through heated steel dies produces constant profiles.
- Repair Scarfing: Grinding shallow bevels into damaged laminate layers restores structural load transfer continuity.

(c) With your counselor, determine how the finished projects will be evaluated. Using those guidelines, evaluate the completed projects with your counselor.
**Source:** *Official Scouting America Composite Materials Merit Badge Pamphlet (p. 60, p. 70)* • **Group Organizer:** Concept & Examples Notes
> **Guide to Safe Scouting Warning:** Cure all mixed resin mixing cups on fireproof concrete surfaces away from combustible shop rags.
> **Starting Gate (Counselor Pre-Approval Required):** Counselor Pre-Approval Required - Review your Composite Materials Req 5 preparation with your Merit Badge Counselor before moving to subsequent tasks.
- **Common Myth:** Adding extra hardener to liquid epoxy speeds up the curing cycle without weakening final laminate strength.
- **Pamphlet Fact:** Insufficient wax or polyvinyl alcohol mold release agent results in permanent mechanical bonding between part and mold.

**Official Pamphlet Summary:**
- Mold Preparation: Applying paste wax and polyvinyl alcohol release film prevents parts from adhering permanently.
- Resin Wetout: Fully saturating woven fibers with squeegees eliminates dry spots and trapped air bubbles.
- Consolidation Pressure: Clamping molds or drawing vacuum consolidates laminate layers and increases fiber volume fraction.
- Tapping cured laminates with plastic inspection hammers reveals internal delaminations and hidden voids.
