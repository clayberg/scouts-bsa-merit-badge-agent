### Req 1: Kayaking Hazards and First Aid
**Official Requirement 1 (Parent Overview):** Do the following: (a) Explain to your counselor the hazards you are most likely to encounter while participating in kayaking activities, including weather and water-related hazards, and what you should do to anticipate, help prevent, mitigate, and respond to these hazards. (b) Review prevention, symptoms, and first-aid treatment for the following injuries or illnesses that can occur while kayaking: blisters, cold-water shock and hypothermia, heat-related illnesses, dehydration, sunburn, sprains, and strains. (c) Review the Scouting America Safety Afloat policy. Explain to your counselor how this applies to kayaking.
**Source:** *Official Scouting America Kayaking Merit Badge Pamphlet (p. 18, p. 20)* • **Group Organizer:** Symptoms, First Aid & Prevention Matrix
> **Guide to Safe Scouting Warning:** Operate under BSA Safe Swim Defense and Safety Afloat for Kayaking Requirement 1: confirm swimmer classification, wear a properly fitted U.S. Coast Guard-approved life jacket when on the water for kayaking hazards and first aid, and maintain buddy checks throughout the session.
> **Starting Gate (Prerequisite Merit Badge / Certification):** BSA Swimmer Classification Gate - Scout must complete the official BSA Swimmer test (100 yards + 1-minute float) before on-water skill stations.
- **Common Myth:** You should leave a painful hand blister completely untouched until your multi-day kayaking trip is over.
- **Pamphlet Fact:** Once a blister forms, draining it immediately relieves pain before you wash it with soap and water, apply antibiotic ointment, and cover it with a dry dressing and waterproof tape (p. 18).

**Official Pamphlet Summary:**
- Once a blister forms, it is best to immediately drain (p. 18).
- Draining the blister usually provides immediate pain relief (p. 18).
- Wash the area with soap and water and then apply a thin layer of an antibiotic ointment to prevent infection (p. 18).
- Cover with a dry dressing and hold it in place with waterproof tape or a compressive elastic bandage (p. 18).

### Req 4: Kayak Anatomy Design and Care
**Official Requirement 4 (Parent Overview):** Do the following: (a) Name and point out the major parts of a kayak. (b) Review the differences in the design between recreational, whitewater, and sea or touring kayaks. Include how length, width, stability, and rocker are involved in the design of each type. (c) Explain the care, maintenance, and storage of a kayak.
**Source:** *Official Scouting America Kayaking Merit Badge Pamphlet (p. 29, p. 35)* • **Group Organizer:** 2-Column Comparison Table
> **Guide to Safe Scouting Warning:** Operate under BSA Safe Swim Defense and Safety Afloat for Kayaking Requirement 4: confirm swimmer classification, wear a properly fitted U.S. Coast Guard-approved life jacket when on the water for kayak anatomy design and care, and maintain buddy checks throughout the session.
- **Common Myth:** A kayak with heavy rocker tracks straighter across a flat lake than a kayak with a flat keel line.
- **Pamphlet Fact:** Heavy rocker curves the bow and stern upward to make a whitewater kayak spin easily, whereas long hulls with minimal rocker track straight across open water (p. 35).

**Official Pamphlet Summary:**
- In selecting a kayak paddle, the most important thing to consider is the comfort of the paddle in your hands (p. 29).
- Quality paddles have an oval shaft, which provides better control and comfort and helps you set the correct angle (p. 29).
- Most paddles have straight shafts, but a few designs are bent at the grip positions to ease strain on the wrists (p. 29).
- Standard Centimeter Paddle Lengths: Paddles for recreational kayaks are normally sized in centimeters, with typical lengths between 200 and 220 centimeters (p. 29).

- **Common Myth:** You should grip both sides of a feathered paddle tightly and twist the shaft inside both palms on every stroke.
- **Pamphlet Fact:** Your control hand maintains a fixed grip aligned with its blade while the shaft rotates loosely inside your opposite hand between strokes (p. 33).

- Knuckle and Blade Alignment: When you hold a paddle, your elbows should be bent at about 90 degrees and the knuckles of your right hand should line up with the edge of your right (p. 33).

### Req 6: Wet Exits and Kayak Rescues
**Official Requirement 6 (Parent Overview):** Using a properly equipped kayak with an open cockpit, a sit-on-top, or an inflatable kayak, do the following: (a) Safely capsize and perform a wet exit. (b) Reenter the kayak with assistance from a buddy boat. (c) Demonstrate a kayak-over-kayak rescue. (d) Demonstrate the HELP position. (e) Capsize the kayak, swim it and the paddle to shore, and empty water from the kayak with assistance if needed.
**Source:** *Official Scouting America Kayaking Merit Badge Pamphlet (p. 56, p. 57)* • **Group Organizer:** Skill Demonstration Checklist
> **Guide to Safe Scouting Warning:** Operate under BSA Safe Swim Defense and Safety Afloat for Kayaking Requirement 6: confirm swimmer classification, wear a properly fitted U.S. Coast Guard-approved life jacket when on the water for wet exits and kayak rescues, and maintain buddy checks throughout the session.
- **Common Myth:** During a kayak-over-kayak rescue, the swimmer should stay right between the two boats to help lift the cockpit.
- **Pamphlet Fact:** To avoid being crushed or injured, the paddler in the water must never get between both kayaks and should instead swim to the far end of the capsized boat (p. 56).

**Official Pamphlet Summary:**
- Avoiding Pinch Zones: To avoid injury, itis important that the paddler in the water never get between both kayaks during this rescue (p. 56).
- Open Water Rescue Needs: If a kayak is capsized far from land or in very cold water, it may not be possible to safely tow the boat to shore (p. 56).
- With practice, akayak can be emptied and then re-boarded in open water using the T-rescue and heel hook entry techniques (p. 56).
- Swimmer Positioning at Stern: The paddler being rescued swims to the far end of the capsized boat (p. 56).

- **Common Myth:** Your legs and feet should stay completely relaxed and loose inside the kayak while your arms do the paddling.
- **Pamphlet Fact:** Anchoring yourself in the kayak by gently pushing with your feet, legs, and buttocks transfers your stroke energy directly into moving the boat (p. 67).

- Ideally, all the energy applied to the paddle should make the boat go where the paddler wants (p. 67).
