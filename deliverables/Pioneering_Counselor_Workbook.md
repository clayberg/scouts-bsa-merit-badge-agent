## 3. Hands-On Skill Station Verification Rubric

- [ ] **Station Req 1:** Do the following: (a) Explain to your counselor the most likely hazards you might encounter while participating in pioneering activities and what you should do to anticipate, help prevent, mitigate, and respond to these hazards. (b) Discuss the prevention of, and first-aid treatment for, injuries and conditions that could occur while working on pioneering projects, including rope splinters, rope burns, cuts, scratches, insect bites and stings, hypothermia, dehydration, heat exhaustion, heatstroke, sunburn, and falls.
- [ ] **Station Req 2:** Do the following: (a) Demonstrate the West Country method of whipping a rope. (b) Demonstrate how to tie a rope tackle and the following knots: clove hitch formed as two half hitches, clove hitch on a bight, butterfly knot, roundturn with two half hitches, and rolling hitch. (c) Demonstrate and explain when to use the following lashings: square, diagonal, round, shear, tripod, and floor lashing.
- [ ] **Station Req 3:** Do the following: (a) Using square and tripod lashings from requirement 2(c), build a Tripod Wash Station (or with your counselor's permission, another camp gadget of your own design). (b) Using rolling hitches or roundturns with two half hitches, and round lashings from requirements 2(b) and 2(c), build a 15-foot Scout Stave Flagpole (or with your counselor's permission, another camp gadget of your own design). (c) Using shear, square, and floor lashings, clove hitches on a bight, and rope tackles from requirements 2(b) and 2(c), build a Simple Camp Table (or with your counselor's permission, another camp gadget of your own design).
- [ ] **Station Req 5:** Explain the uses for the back splice, eye splice, and short splice. View a demonstration on forming each splice.
- [ ] **Station Req 9:** Working in a group, (or individually with the help of your counselor) build a full size pioneering structure, using one of the following designs in the Pioneering merit badge pamphlet: Double A-Frame Monkey Bridge, Single A-Frame Bridge, Single Trestle Bridge, Single Lock Bridge, 4x4 Square Climbing Tower, Four Flag Gateway Tower, Double Tripod Chippewa Kitchen, or another type of structure approved in advance by your counselor. Carefully plan the project, assembling and organizing all the materials, referring to the points under Safe Pioneering, and complying with the height restrictions in the Guide to Safe Scouting.

### Req 4: Cordage Characteristics and Selection
**Official Requirement 4:** Explain the differences between synthetic ropes and natural-fiber ropes. Discuss which types of rope are suitable for pioneering work and why. Include the following in your discussion: breaking strength, safe working loads, and the care and storage of rope.
**Source:** *Official Scouting America Pioneering Merit Badge Pamphlet (p. 9, p. 10)* • **Scout Workbook Organizer (2-Column Comparison Table):** Req 4 Technical Comparison Matrix
> **Guide to Safe Scouting Warning:** Retire any rope that shows internal powdering, acidic chemical stains, or melted synthetic strands.
- **Socratic Prompt (Ask Scouts First):** Why is rope stretch dangerous when tensioning monkey bridge foot ropes?
- **Official Pamphlet Summary & Expected Answer Key:**
  - Natural manila fibers bite together under load, preventing lashing wraps from slipping.
  - Synthetic nylon stretches thirty percent, storing kinetic energy that causes sudden whiplash.
  - Safe working loads must remain below one fifth of total breaking strength.
  - Cordage Storage Care: Store natural fiber ropes coiled in dry, well ventilated lockers to stop mildew.
- **Step-by-Step Facilitation & Station Procedure:**
  - Step 1 - Material Assessment: Check rope tags to verify natural manila construction before rigging structural joints.
  - Step 2 - Load Calculation: Divide catalog breaking strength by five to establish the safe working limit.
  - Step 3 - Strand Examination: Untwist outer strands gently to check internal fibers for discoloration or grit.
  - Step 4 - Proper Coiling: Coil working lines clockwise into clean loops to prevent kinks during storage.
- **Common Myth:** Nylon rope is superior for pioneering towers because it has higher breaking strength.
- **Pamphlet Fact:** Nylon stretches excessively under load, loosening lashings and destabilizing elevated tower platforms.

### Req 5: Permanent Rope Splicing Techniques
**Official Requirement 5:** Explain the uses for the back splice, eye splice, and short splice. View a demonstration on forming each splice.
**Source:** *Official Scouting America Pioneering Merit Badge Pamphlet (p. 52, p. 53)* • **Scout Workbook Organizer (Concept & Examples Notes):** Req 5 Structured Technical Study & Explanation Matrix
> **Guide to Safe Scouting Warning:** Never trust a splice that has fewer than three full tuck cycles per strand.
- **Socratic Prompt (Ask Scouts First):** Why does an eye splice retain more strength than a bowline knot?
- **Official Pamphlet Summary & Expected Answer Key:**
  - Eye splices retain ninety percent of rope strength while forming permanent terminal loops.
  - Back splices begin with a crown knot to redirect strands down the line.
  - Short splices double rope diameter at the joint, preventing passage through standard blocks.
  - Strand Tucking Rule: Three full tucks against rope lay ensure maximum friction against strand pullout.
- **Step-by-Step Facilitation & Station Procedure:**
  - Step 1 - Strand Unlaying: Unlay three strand ends five turns and tape tips to prevent unravelling.
  - Step 2 - Core Positioning: Form the desired eye diameter and marry working strands across standing lines.
  - Step 3 - Friction Weaving: Tuck each working strand over one standing strand and under the next.
  - Step 4 - Joint Tapering: Complete three tuck cycles, roll underfoot, and trim excess strand ends neatly.
- **Common Myth:** Tying a knot in rope is just as strong as completing a proper splice.
- **Pamphlet Fact:** Knots reduce rope breaking strength up to fifty percent, while splices retain ninety percent.

**Official Requirement 8:** Describe the lashings that are used when building a trestle, how the poles are positioned, and how X braces contribute to the overall structural integrity of a pioneering project.

- **Socratic Prompt (Ask Scouts First):** Why do the two diagonal X braces sit on opposite faces of the trestle legs?
- **Official Pamphlet Summary & Expected Answer Key:**
  - Trestle Leg Symmetry: Legs must form an isosceles trapezoid to distribute downward weight evenly across ground.
  - Ledger Attachment Points: Square lashings secure horizontal ledgers firmly beneath the trestle legs to bear loads.
  - Diagonal Cross Bracing: X braces attached to opposite pole faces prevent racking and torsional frame twisting.
  - Center Intersection Binding: A diagonal lashing binds crossing X braces where they spring apart under stress.
- **Step-by-Step Facilitation & Station Procedure:**
  - Step 1 - Spar Alignment: Lay two trestle legs parallel, spacing them wider at the base than top.
  - Step 2 - Ledger Lashing: Secure top and bottom horizontal ledgers across legs using tight square lashings.
  - Step 3 - Diagonal Positioning: Place two cross braces diagonally across opposite sides of the rectangular frame.
  - Step 4 - Joint Locking: Lash four brace ends with square lashings and the center with diagonal lashing.
- **Common Myth:** A simple rectangular frame with four square corners supports heavy pioneering bridge decks.
- **Pamphlet Fact:** Rectangular frames rack instantly without diagonal X braces forming rigid structural triangles.
