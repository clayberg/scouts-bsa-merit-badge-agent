### Req 1: Canoeing Hazards and First Aid
**Official Requirement 1 (Parent Overview):** Do the following: (a) Review with your counselor the most likely hazards you may encounter while participating in canoeing activities and what you should do to anticipate, help prevent, mitigate, and respond to these hazards. (b) Review prevention, symptoms, and first-aid treatment for the following injuries or illnesses that could occur while canoeing: blisters, cold-water shock and hypothermia, dehydration, heat-related illnesses, sunburn, sprains, and strains. (c) Discuss the Scouting America Safety Afloat policy. Tell how it applies to canoeing activities.
**Source:** *Official Scouting America Canoeing Merit Badge Pamphlet (p. 16, p. 17)* • **Group Organizer:** Symptoms, First Aid & Prevention Matrix
> **Guide to Safe Scouting Warning:** Operate under BSA Safe Swim Defense and Safety Afloat for Canoeing Requirement 1: confirm swimmer classification, wear a properly fitted U.S. Coast Guard-approved life jacket when on the water for canoeing hazards and first aid, and maintain buddy checks throughout the session.
> **Starting Gate (Prerequisite Merit Badge / Certification):** BSA Swimmer Classification Gate - Scout must complete the official BSA Swimmer test (100 yards + 1-minute float) before on-water skill stations.
- **Common Myth:** Following the nine points of Safety Afloat completely eliminates any chance of getting injured during a canoe trip.
- **Pamphlet Fact:** Following the nine points of Safety Afloat reduces many serious risks in canoeing, though major and minor injuries can still occur on the water (p. 16).

**Official Pamphlet Summary:**
- Because canoeing is a physical activity that takes place in the outdoors, participants are at risk for arange of injuries (p. 16).
- Following the nine points of Safety Afloat reduces many of the serious risks in canoeing, but some major and minor injuries still might occur (p. 16).
- First aid for some of the temperature-related injuries can be lifesaving while treatment for other conditions is less urgent (p. 16).
- There are two primary cold related dangers encountered in canoeing (p. 16).

#### Req 1a: Anticipating and Mitigating Hazards
**Official Requirement 1a:** Review with your counselor the most likely hazards you may encounter while participating in canoeing activities and what you should do to anticipate, help prevent, mitigate, and respond to these hazards.
**Source:** *Official Scouting America Canoeing Merit Badge Pamphlet (p. 16, p. 17)* • **Organizer (Concept & Examples Notes):** Req 1a Structured Technical Study & Explanation Matrix (Canoeing Hazards and First Aid)
> **Starting Gate (Prerequisite Merit Badge / Certification):** BSA Swimmer Classification Gate - Scout must complete the official BSA Swimmer test (100 yards + 1-minute float) before on-water skill stations.
- **Common Myth:** Hypothermia strikes within seconds the moment a paddler falls out of a canoe into cold water.
- **Pamphlet Fact:** Sudden immersion triggers immediate cold-water shock, whereas hypothermia is a gradual lowering of the core body temperature over minutes to hours in cold water (p. 16).

- [ ] BSA Safe Swim Defense / Safety Afloat buddy tags and watercraft inspection checklist
- [ ] Bilge pump or bailer, spare paddle/oar, painter lines, and dry bag with thermal layer

### Req 3: Canoe Anatomy and Hull Design
**Official Requirement 3 (Parent Overview):** Do the following: (a) Name and point out the major parts of a canoe. (b) Describe how the length and shape of a canoe affect its performance.
**Source:** *Official Scouting America Canoeing Merit Badge Pamphlet (p. 27, p. 28)* • **Group Organizer:** Concept & Examples Notes
> **Guide to Safe Scouting Warning:** Operate under BSA Safe Swim Defense and Safety Afloat for Canoeing Requirement 3: confirm swimmer classification, wear a properly fitted U.S. Coast Guard-approved life jacket when on the water for canoe anatomy and hull design, and maintain buddy checks throughout the session.
- **Common Myth:** Adding a center keel along the bottom of a canoe makes the boat spin and turn much more easily in tight corners.
- **Pamphlet Fact:** A keel improves a canoe's ability to track in a straight line across open water, but it actually hinders the boat's ability to turn quickly (p. 27).

**Official Pamphlet Summary:**
- Gunwales (pronounced "gunnels") are rails that run along the top edge of both sides of the canoe (p. 27).
- Hull Strength and Shape Retention: Gunwales add strength to the hull and help it keep its shape (p. 27).
- Braces, called thwarts (pronounced "thorts"), span the width of the canoe and provide rigidity and support (p. 27).
- Some canoes also have a small keel, a ridge that runs the length of the bottom of the canoe along its center line (p. 27).

#### Req 3a: Major Parts of a Canoe

- **Pamphlet Fact:** Gunwales are the rails running along the top edges of both sides of the hull, while the crosswise braces spanning the width are called thwarts (p. 27).

### Req 4: Paddle Anatomy and Proper Sizing
**Official Requirement 4 (Parent Overview):** Do the following: (a) Name and point out the parts of a paddle. (b) Demonstrate how to correctly size a paddle for a paddler in a sitting position and a kneeling position.
**Source:** *Official Scouting America Canoeing Merit Badge Pamphlet (p. 38, p. 43)* • **Group Organizer:** Skill Demonstration Checklist
> **Guide to Safe Scouting Warning:** Operate under BSA Safe Swim Defense and Safety Afloat for Canoeing Requirement 4: confirm swimmer classification, wear a properly fitted U.S. Coast Guard-approved life jacket when on the water for paddle anatomy and proper sizing, and maintain buddy checks throughout the session.
- **Common Myth:** When kneeling in a canoe, you should hover upright on your knees without touching the seat or thwart behind you.
- **Pamphlet Fact:** When kneeling in a canoe, a paddler should sit up straight with their buttocks leaning against the seat or thwart behind them to form a stable base (p. 38).

**Official Pamphlet Summary:**
- In the kneeling position, the paddler's center of gravity is lower than in the seated position (p. 38).
- Increased Stability When Kneeling: This puts the paddler in a more stable position in the boat (p. 38).
- Leaning Against Seat or Thwart: When kneeling in the canoe, the paddler should sit up straight with their buttocks leaning against the seat or thwart behind them (p. 38).
- Proper Back and Leg Alignment: Avoid kneeling with the back and upper legs in alignment (p. 38).

#### Req 4a: Parts of a Canoe Paddle
**Official Requirement 4a:** Name and point out the parts of a paddle.

- **Pamphlet Fact:** Kneeling lowers the paddler's center of gravity inside the hull, putting the boat in a much more stable position than sitting (p. 38).

### Req 7: Launching, Landing, and Boarding
**Official Requirement 7 (Parent Overview):** With a companion, use a properly equipped canoe to demonstrate the following: (a) Safely carry and launch the canoe from a dock or shore (both, if possible). (b) Safely land the canoe on a dock or shore (both, if possible) and return it to its proper storage location. (c) Demonstrate kneeling and sitting positions in a canoe and explain the proper use for each position. (d) Change places while afloat in the canoe.
**Source:** *Official Scouting America Canoeing Merit Badge Pamphlet (p. 37, p. 45)* • **Group Organizer:** Skill Demonstration Checklist
> **Guide to Safe Scouting Warning:** Operate under BSA Safe Swim Defense and Safety Afloat for Canoeing Requirement 7: confirm swimmer classification, wear a properly fitted U.S. Coast Guard-approved life jacket when on the water for launching, landing, and boarding, and maintain buddy checks throughout the session.
- **Common Myth:** Pulling half of the canoe onto the bank while leaving the stern floating makes the boat steadier to climb into.
- **Pamphlet Fact:** You must never bridge a canoe with one end on shore and the other floating, because stepping into a bridged hull is unstable and can injure the paddler or damage the boat (p. 37).

**Official Pamphlet Summary:**
- Teamwork and Launch Communication: Moving a canoe from where it is stored on land to where it is to be launched and then safely getting into it requires teamwork and good communication (p. 37).
- Most canoeing injuries on flatwater take place while carrying, entering, or exiting the canoe (p. 37).

### Req 9: Tandem Strokes and Maneuvers
**Official Requirement 9 (Parent Overview):** With a companion, using a properly equipped canoe to demonstrate the following tandem maneuvers while paddling in unison on opposite sides and without changing sides. Each paddler must demonstrate these maneuvers in both the bow and stern and on both sides of the canoe while maintaining trim and balance of the canoe: (a) Pivot (spin) the canoe 180° (half circle) to the right from a stationary position, stop, and return to the starting position by pivoting to the left using draw and pushaway strokes or using forward and reverse sweeps staying within 2 boat lengths of the starting position. (c) While in forward motion, stop the canoe within two boat lengths using the backstroke. (d) While in the stern position and without assistance from the bow paddler, paddle in a straight line 15-20 boat lengths using an appropriate steering stroke, e. g., the J-stroke or thumb-down rudder stroke with or without a stern pry. (e) Move the canoe in a straight line 15-20 boat lengths with the bow paddler using as necessary the forward stroke, draw stroke or forward sweep and the stern paddler using an appropriate steering stroke, e. g., the J-stroke or thumb-down rudder stroke with or without a stern pry. (f) While maintaining forward motion, turn the canoe 90° in an arc to the right in 5-10 boat lengths with the bow paddler using as necessary either the draw stroke or forward sweep and the stern paddler using only the forward stroke. Repeat the maneuver turning the canoe 90° to the left. (g) Move the canoe backwards in a straight line 3-4 boat lengths using as necessary the back stroke, reverse sweep or draw stroke in the bow or the stern. (h) Complete a figure of 8 course around markers 3-4 boat lengths apart using appropriate strokes including the draw stroke, and the forward and reverse sweeps.
