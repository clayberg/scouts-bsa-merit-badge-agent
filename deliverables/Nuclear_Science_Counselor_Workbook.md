## 2. Pre-Class Prerequisite Checklist ('Bring Proof to Class')

- [ ] **Req 3 (Explain):** Modern Particle Physics. Do the following: (a) Explain how a particle accelerator works. (b) Do ONE of the following: (1) Visit an accelerator, research lab, or university and discuss with a scientist how they study the properties of the nucleus or nucleons. (2) List three particle accelerators and describe several experiments conducted and their associated basic science and practical applications.
- [ ] **Req 5 (Explain):** Radiation Safety. Do ONE of the following: (a) Using a radiation survey meter and a radioactive source, show how the counts per minute change as the source gets closer to or farther from the radiation detector. Adults must handle radioactive materials. Place three different materials between the source and the detector, then explain any differences in the measurements per minute. Explain how time, distance, and shielding can reduce an individual's radiation dose. (b) Describe how radon is detected in homes. Discuss the steps taken for the long-term and short-term test methods, tell how to interpret the results, and explain when each type of test should be used. Explain the health concerns related to radon gas and tell what steps can be taken to reduce radon in buildings. (c) Visit a place where X-rays are used. Draw a floor plan of this room. Show where the unit, the unit operator, and the patient would be when the X-ray unit is operated. Explain the precautions taken and the importance of those precautions for the safety of the operator.
- [ ] **Req 6 (Discuss):** Nuclear Energy. Do ONE of the following; then discuss with your counselor how nuclear energy is used to produce electricity: (a) Make a drawing showing how nuclear fission happens. Observe a mousetrap reactor (setup by an adult) and use it to explain how a chain reaction could be started. Explain how a chain reaction could be stopped or controlled in a nuclear reactor. Explain what is meant by a "critical mass." (b) Visit a local nuclear power plant or nuclear reactor either in person or online (with your parent or guardian's permission). Learn how a reactor works and how the plant generates electricity. Find out what percentage of electricity in the United States and in your state is generated by nuclear power plants and by other methods. Make a graph of the information you find.
- [ ] **Req 8 (Prepare):** Careers. Explore careers related to nuclear science. Research one career to learn about the training and education needed, costs, job prospects, salary, job duties, and career advancement. Your research methods may include, with your parent or guardian's permission, an internet or library search, an interview with a professional in the field, or a visit to a location where people in this career work. Discuss with your counselor both your findings and what about this profession might make it an interesting career.

**Official Requirement 1 (Parent Overview):** Radiation. Do the following: (a) Explain radiation and the difference between ionizing and nonionizing radiation. (b) Explain the ALARA principle. Explain what process you would go through to ensure your dose is kept ALARA when performing the requirements in this merit badge. (c) Describe the radiation hazard symbol and explain where it should be used. (d) Explain how we are exposed to ionizing radiation from outside the earth as well as on earth every day. List four examples of Naturally Occurring Radioactive Materials (NORM) that are in your house or grocery store and explain why they are radioactive. (e) Explain the difference between radiation exposure and contamination. Describe the hazards of radiation to humans, the environment, and wildlife. Calculate your approximate annual radiation dose and compare it to a typical expected dose of someone who works in a nuclear power plant.
**Source:** *Official Scouting America Nuclear Science Merit Badge Pamphlet (p. 67, p. 69)* • **Group Organizer:** 2-Column Comparison Table
> **Guide to Safe Scouting Warning:** BSA Youth Protection & Buddy System (Req 1): Complete all Radiation Principles and ALARA Safety discussions and demonstrations with Two-Deep Leadership and your patrol buddy.
- **Common Myth:** Getting a medical X-ray at the doctor's office makes your body radioactive afterward.
- **Pamphlet Fact:** Being exposed to radiation such as a medical X-ray does not make a person radioactive, just as shining a flashlight on your hand does not make it glow in the dark (p. 67).

**Official Pamphlet Summary:**
- Radiation Effects on Wildlife: Although we know much about the dangers of radiation to humans, its effects on wildlife are less understood (p. 67).
- Past Protection Assumptions: Scientists have tended to assume that as long as people were protected, animals and plants would be, too (p. 67).
- But that idea is changing, and more research is now being done to learn how best to safeguard wildlife (p. 67).
- Reactor Release Field Studies: Many researchers are focusing on how wild plants and animals have been affected by the radioactivity released from the exploded nuclear reactor (p. 67).

- **Common Myth:** Different isotopes of the same element have different numbers of protons in their nuclei.
- **Pamphlet Fact:** All isotopes of a given element have the exact same number of protons, which defines the element, but they contain different numbers of neutrons (p. 12).

**Official Pamphlet Summary:**
- Begin by studying pictures of the three isotopes of hydrogen (p. 12).
- Ordinary hydrogen has one proton, one electron, and no neutrons (p. 12).
- Deuterium (written in isotope notation as 2D) with 1 proton and 1 neutron, andtritium (3T or 3H) with 1 proton (p. 12).

### Req 3: Particle Accelerators and Subatomic Research
**Official Requirement 3 (Parent Overview):** Modern Particle Physics. Do the following: (a) Explain how a particle accelerator works. (b) Do ONE of the following: (1) Visit an accelerator, research lab, or university and discuss with a scientist how they study the properties of the nucleus or nucleons. (2) List three particle accelerators and describe several experiments conducted and their associated basic science and practical applications.
**Source:** *Official Scouting America Nuclear Science Merit Badge Pamphlet (p. 23, p. 24)* • **Group Organizer:** Concept & Examples Notes
> **Guide to Safe Scouting Warning:** Conduct all counselor discussions, community interviews, and research for Nuclear Science Requirement 3 on particle accelerators and subatomic research under BSA Youth Protection two-deep leadership, copying a parent or guardian on any external correspondence.
- **Common Myth:** Particle accelerators use mechanical fans or high-pressure gas nozzles to blow neutral atoms down a pipe.
- **Pamphlet Fact:** Particle accelerators use powerful electric fields to speed up electrically charged particles and magnetic fields to steer and focus them into a tight beam (p. 23).

**Official Pamphlet Summary:**
- Particle accelerators, sometimes called atom smashers, are machines that can create beams of electrically charged particles and accelerate them (p. 23).
- This acceleration increases the particles' energy of motion (p. 23).
- Fastball Energy Analogy: Think of the difference between catching a baseball that is gently underhanded to you and catching a fast ball fired at you by a major-league pitcher (p. 23).
- Energy is the universal container that holds things together (p. 23).

#### Req 3a: A particle accelerator works
**Official Requirement 3a:** Explain how a particle accelerator works.

> **Guide to Safe Scouting Warning:** BSA Youth Protection & Buddy System (Req 4): Complete all Classic Nuclear Physics Experiments discussions and demonstrations with Two-Deep Leadership and your patrol buddy.
- **Common Myth:** When you look into a cloud chamber, you are seeing the actual alpha and beta particles glowing in the dark.
- **Pamphlet Fact:** Subatomic particles are far too small to see directly; a cloud chamber reveals the trails of tiny alcohol droplets that condense along the path of ions left behind by the particles (p. 33).

**Official Pamphlet Summary:**
- Although ionizing particles are invisible, you can use a cloud chamber tosee the tracks the particles leave (p. 33).
- Buy dry ice the morning of the demonstration in order to minimize the sublimation losses of the dry ice (p. 33).
- Venting Carbon Dioxide Safely: If you must buy the evening before, store in a sealed cooler that allows the CO2 gas to vent as it sublimes from the solid form (p. 33).
- Place as trip of felt on the inside of the bottom rim (p. 33).
