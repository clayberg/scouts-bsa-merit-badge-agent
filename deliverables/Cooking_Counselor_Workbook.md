### Req 1: Kitchen Safety and Food Sanitation
**Official Requirement 1 (Parent Overview):** Health and safety. Do the following: (a) Explain to your counselor the most likely hazards you may encounter while participating in cooking activities and what you should do to anticipate, help prevent, mitigate, and respond to these hazards. (b) Show that you know first aid for and how to prevent injuries or illnesses that could occur while preparing meals and eating, including burns and scalds, cuts, choking, and allergic reactions. (c) Describe how meat, fish, chicken, eggs, dairy products, and fresh vegetables should be stored, transported, and properly prepared for cooking. Explain how to prevent cross-contamination. (d) Discuss with your counselor food allergies, food intolerance, and food-related illnesses and diseases. Explain why someone who handles or prepares food needs to be aware of these concerns. (e) Discuss with your counselor why reading food labels is important. Explain how to identify common allergens such as peanuts, tree nuts, milk, eggs, wheat, soy, and shellfish.
**Source:** *Official Scouting America Cooking Merit Badge Pamphlet (p. 64, p. 71)* • **Group Organizer:** 5-Column Hazard Assessment Matrix
> **Guide to Safe Scouting Warning:** Review task hazards with your counselor before starting Cooking Requirement 1 on kitchen safety and food sanitation: inspect tools and work surfaces before use, keep bystanders clear of active demonstrations, and follow BSA Guide to Safe Scouting supervision standards.
- **Common Myth:** Rinsing raw poultry in the sink removes surface bacteria and makes the meat safer to cook.
- **Pamphlet Fact:** Washing raw meat or poultry splashes bacteria onto surrounding counters and utensils, increasing cross-contamination risk instead of killing pathogens through thorough cooking (p. 64).

**Official Pamphlet Summary:**
- Safety is always a Scout's primary consideration (p. 64).
- Emergency Injury Readiness: All good cooks should be prepared to take action should an injury occur (p. 64).
- Cross-Contamination Prevention: They must know how to properly prepare and store foods to help prevent cross-contamination and avoid the spread of food-related illnesses (p. 64).
- Recognizing Allergic Reactions: They should also be able to identify when individuals become sick because of an illness or allergic reaction (p. 64).

**Source:** *Official Scouting America Cooking Merit Badge Pamphlet (p. 54, p. 61)* • **Group Organizer:** Calorie Log 5Day
> **Guide to Safe Scouting Warning:** BSA Youth Protection & Buddy System (Req 2): Complete all Daily Nutrition and MyPlate Guidelines discussions and demonstrations with Two-Deep Leadership and your patrol buddy.
- **Common Myth:** All teenagers of the same age need the exact same number of daily calories regardless of how much they exercise.
- **Pamphlet Fact:** Daily caloric and food group needs vary significantly by age, sex, and daily physical activity duration under USDA guidelines (p. 54).

**Official Pamphlet Summary:**
- Active Youth Calorie Needs: According to the USDA's daily food plans, the average 12-year-old male who exercises 30 to 60 minutes per day should consume about 2,200 calories a day (p. 54).
- Balanced Daily Food Groups: He should include, on average, 7 ounces of grains, 3 cups of vegetables, 2 cups of fruit, 3 cups from the dairy group, and 6 ounces from the protein (p. 54).
- Activity Level Adjustments: A 15-year-old male who is physically active less than 30 minutes a day should follow the same daily food plan as the 12-year-old male (p. 54).
- Tailored Daily Calorie Targets: The average 12-year-old female who exercises 30 to 60 minutes per day should consume about 2,000 calories a day (p. 54).

### Req 3: Fundamental Cooking Methods and Equipment

For each one, describe the equipment needed, how temperature control is maintained, and name at least one food that can be cooked using that method: baking, boiling, broiling, pan frying, simmering, microwaving, air frying, grilling, foil cooking, and Dutch oven. (b) Discuss the benefits of using a camp stove on an outing vs. a charcoal or wood fire. (c) Describe for your counselor how to manage your time when preparing a meal so components for each course are ready to serve at the correct time. (d) Explain and give examples of how taste, texture, and smell impact what we eat.
**Source:** *Official Scouting America Cooking Merit Badge Pamphlet (p. 24, p. 36)* • **Group Organizer:** 2-Column Comparison Table
> **Guide to Safe Scouting Warning:** BSA Youth Protection & Buddy System (Req 3): Complete all Fundamental Cooking Methods and Equipment discussions and demonstrations with Two-Deep Leadership and your patrol buddy.
- **Common Myth:** Boiling and simmering are the exact same cooking method, just done in different sizes of pots.
- **Pamphlet Fact:** Boiling cooks food at 212 degrees Fahrenheit with vigorous rolling bubbles, whereas simmering uses gentler heat just below boiling so delicate foods do not break apart (p. 24).

**Official Pamphlet Summary:**
- Dutch Oven Camp Cooking: Some of the tastiest meals you will make and eat as a Scout will be cooked in a Dutch oven (p. 24).
- Its thick build produces an even heat, ideal for slow simmering (p. 24).
- Camp Kitchen Cookware Versatility: Despite its weight, the Dutch oven'sversatility makes it a valuable tool in any camp kitchen (p. 24).
- Base Camp Cookware Selection: If carrying or moving heavy equipment is not a problem, the Dutch oven is excellent for cooking many dishes (p. 24).
