### Req 1: Carving Hazards and First Aid
**Official Requirement 1 (Parent Overview):** Do the following: (a) Explain to your counselor the hazards you are most likely to encounter while wood carving, and what you should do to anticipate, help prevent, mitigate, or lessen these hazards. (b) Show that you know first aid for injuries that could occur while wood carving, including minor cuts and scratches and splinters.
**Source:** *Official Scouting America Wood Carving Merit Badge Pamphlet (p. 15, p. 26)* • **Group Organizer:** Concept & Examples Notes
> **Guide to Safe Scouting Warning:** Review task hazards with your counselor before starting Wood Carving Requirement 1 on carving hazards and first aid: inspect tools and work surfaces before use, keep bystanders clear of active demonstrations, and follow BSA Guide to Safe Scouting supervision standards.
- **Common Myth:** A dull carving blade is safer for beginners because it cannot cut skin as deeply as a honed edge.
- **Pamphlet Fact:** Dull blades actually cause more carving injuries because they require excessive hand force and are far more likely to slip off the wood (p. 15).

**Official Pamphlet Summary:**
- Preventing Cuts and Nicks: Prevention is the best way to avoid cuts, nicks, and other injuries (p. 15).
- Preparing for Minor Injuries: However, as a Scout working with wood and knives, you should be prepared in the event of a cut or perhaps a splinter (p. 15).
- Notifying the Supervising Adult: If you are injured, stay calm and let the adult in charge know what has happened (p. 15).
- Expecting the Unexpected: Sometimes accidents happen even when all safety precautions are taken (p. 15).

- **Common Myth:** Brand-new wood carving knives come from the factory completely ready to carve without any sharpening.

### Req 4: Fundamental Wood Carving Cuts
**Official Requirement 4 (Parent Overview):** Using a piece of scrap wood or a project on which you are working, show your counselor that you know how to do the following: (a) Paring cut (b) Basic cut and push cut (c) V cut (d) Stop cut or score line
**Source:** *Official Scouting America Wood Carving Merit Badge Pamphlet (p. 28, p. 39)* • **Group Organizer:** Concept & Examples Notes
> **Guide to Safe Scouting Warning:** Review task hazards with your counselor before starting Wood Carving Requirement 4 on fundamental wood carving cuts: inspect tools and work surfaces before use, keep bystanders clear of active demonstrations, and follow BSA Guide to Safe Scouting supervision standards.
- **Common Myth:** A paring cut is dangerous because the blade faces your thumb, so you should pull the knife with your whole arm.
- **Pamphlet Fact:** In a proper paring cut, you anchor your thumb on the wood as a pivot and squeeze with your fingers so the blade cannot travel past your protected thumb (p. 39).

**Official Pamphlet Summary:**
- This cut is away from you, taking small pieces of wood (p. 28).
- By placing the opposite thumb on the back of the blade, you are creating a lever and making it easier to remove the wood from your project (p. 28).
- Preventing Accidental Over-Cutting: The stop cut helps prevent over cutting by giving you a place toend the cut (p. 28).
- Setting Ninety-Degree Boundaries: The stop cut is usually 90 degrees from the maincut (p. 28).

### Req 5: Selecting Woods for Carving Projects
**Official Requirement 5:** Tell why different woods are used for different projects. Explain why you chose the type of wood you did for your projects in requirements 6 and 7.
**Source:** *Official Scouting America Wood Carving Merit Badge Pamphlet (p. 29, p. 43)* • **Organizer (Concept & Examples Notes):** Req 5 Structured Technical Study & Explanation Matrix (Woods Are Used for Different)
> **Guide to Safe Scouting Warning:** BSA Youth Protection & Buddy System (Req 5): Complete all Selecting Woods for Carving Projects discussions and demonstrations with Two-Deep Leadership and your patrol buddy.
- **Common Myth:** Oak and yellow pine from a hardware store are ideal woods for a beginner's first hand-carving project.
- **Pamphlet Fact:** Oak is very hard and coarse-grained, and yellow pine has alternating hard and soft growth rings that deflect knives, making soft, even-grained basswood far better for beginners (p. 43).
