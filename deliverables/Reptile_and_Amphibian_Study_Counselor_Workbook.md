### Req 5: Reptile and Amphibian Reproductive Strategies
**Official Requirement 5:** Compare how reptiles reproduce to how amphibians reproduce.
**Source:** *Official Scouting America Reptile and Amphibian Study Merit Badge Pamphlet (p. 6, p. 7)* • **Organizer (2-Column Comparison Table):** Req 5 Technical Comparison Matrix (Reptiles Reproduce to How Amphibians)
> **Guide to Safe Scouting Warning:** Follow BSA Guide to Safe Scouting field protocols for Reptile and Amphibian Study Requirement 5: travel with a buddy, carry the Scout Basic Essentials and water while studying reptile and amphibian reproductive strategies, and respect wildlife distance and Leave No Trace guidelines.
- **Common Myth:** All reptiles lay hard-shelled eggs on land, while all amphibians give birth to miniature adults in water.
- **Pamphlet Fact:** Most amphibians lay shell-less, jelly-coated eggs in water that hatch into gill-breathing larvae before undergoing metamorphosis, whereas reptiles use internal fertilization and either lay leathery or calcareous amniotic eggs on land or give live birth to fully formed young (p. 6).

### Req 6: Biomechanics of Snake Locomotion
**Official Requirement 6:** From observation, describe how snakes move forward. Describe the functions of the muscles, ribs, and belly plates. Resources: How Snakes Move! (They Don't Just Slither!) (video) How Do Snakes Move? (video)
**Source:** *Official Scouting America Reptile and Amphibian Study Merit Badge Pamphlet (p. 7, p. 8)* • **Organizer (Concept & Examples Notes):** Req 6 Structured Technical Study & Explanation Matrix (Observation Describe How Snakes Move)
> **Guide to Safe Scouting Warning:** BSA Youth Protection & Buddy System (Req 6): Complete all Biomechanics of Snake Locomotion discussions and demonstrations with Two-Deep Leadership and your patrol buddy.
- **Common Myth:** Snakes walk forward inside their skin by using their ribs like dozens of tiny rigid legs.

### Req 7: Venomous Species Identification and First Aid
**Official Requirement 7:** Describe in detail six venomous snakes and the one venomous lizard found in the United States. Describe their habits and geographic range. Tell what you should do in case of a bite by a venomous species.
**Source:** *Official Scouting America Reptile and Amphibian Study Merit Badge Pamphlet (p. 7, p. 8)* • **Organizer (Concept & Examples Notes):** Req 7 Structured Technical Study & Explanation Matrix (Detail Six Venomous Snakes)
> **Guide to Safe Scouting Warning:** BSA Youth Protection & Buddy System (Req 7): Complete all Venomous Species Identification and First Aid discussions and demonstrations with Two-Deep Leadership and your patrol buddy.
- **Common Myth:** If someone is bitten by a venomous snake, you should cut an X over the fang marks and suck out the venom or apply a tight tourniquet.
- **Pamphlet Fact:** Cutting the wound, sucking venom, applying ice, or tying a tourniquet causes severe tissue damage, whereas proper first aid requires keeping the victim calm, immobilizing the bitten limb below heart level, and getting immediate emergency medical transport (p. 7).

### Req 8: Long-Term Care and Behavioral Observation
**Official Requirement 8 (Parent Overview):** Do ONE of the following: (a) Take custody of one or more reptiles or amphibians in a manner approved by your counselor. Maintain one or more reptiles or amphibians for at least a month. Record food accepted, eating methods, changes in coloration, shedding of skins, and general habits; or keep the eggs of a reptile from the time of laying until hatching; or keep the eggs of an amphibian from the time of laying until their transformation into tadpoles (frogs) or larvae (salamanders). Whichever you choose, keep records of and report to your counselor how you cared for your animal, eggs, or larvae, including lighting, habitat, temperature and humidity maintenance, and any veterinary care requirements. Unless you are the long-term owner, at the conclusion of this study, turn the animal(s) over to another responsible party approved by your counselor. (b) Choose a reptile or amphibian that you can observe or foster at a local zoo, aquarium, nature center, local rescue, or other such exhibit (such as your classroom or school). Study the specimen weekly for a period of three months. At each visit, sketch the specimen in its captive habitat and note any changes in its coloration, shedding of skins, and general habits and behavior. Discuss with your counselor how the animal you observed was cared for to include its housing and habitat, how the lighting, temperature, and humidity were maintained, and any veterinary care requirements. Find out, either from information you locate on your own or by talking to the caretaker, what this species eats and what are its native habitat and home range, preferred climate, average life expectancy, and natural predators. Also, identify any human-caused threats to its population and any laws that protect the species and its habitat. After the observation period, share what you have learned with your counselor.
**Source:** *Official Scouting America Reptile and Amphibian Study Merit Badge Pamphlet (p. 6, p. 8)* • **Group Organizer:** Field Sketch & Diagram Canvas
