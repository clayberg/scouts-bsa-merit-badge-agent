### Req 1: Game Analysis and Play Value
**Official Requirement 1 (Parent Overview):** Do the following: (a) Analyze four games you have played, each from a different medium. Identify the medium, player format, objectives, rules, resources, and theme (if relevant). Discuss with your counselor the play experience, what you enjoy in each game, and what you dislike. Make a chart to compare and contrast the games. (b) Describe four types of play value and provide an example of a game built around each concept. Discuss with your counselor other reasons people play games.
**Source:** *Official Scouting America Game Design Merit Badge Pamphlet (p. 38, p. 45)* • **Group Organizer:** 2-Column Comparison Table
> **Guide to Safe Scouting Warning:** Apply BSA Digital Safety and Cyber Chip guidelines during Game Design Requirement 1: protect personal credentials and private data while working on game analysis and play value, verify software sources before downloading, and include a parent or counselor on all online communications.
- **Common Myth:** Every successful game relies on a single type of play value to keep players entertained from start to finish.
- **Pamphlet Fact:** Most engaging games combine multiple types of play value, such as blending strategic challenge with fast-paced threat or story-driven fantasy (p. 38).

**Official Pamphlet Summary:**
- Skill Mastery and Exercise: Other motivations to play are to get exercise, master a skill, and teach someone else how to play (p. 38).
- Reflexes and Threat Appeal: Games that incorporate a high degree of reflex into their game mechanics appeal to the player's desire for threat (p. 38).
- Forms of Player Threat: Tension, danger, provocation, and humiliation all are ways that players experience threat (p. 38).
- Fast Action and Competition: These games are fun because of their heart-pounding action and the enjoyment people get out of crushing their opponents (p. 38).

### Req 4: Modifying Rules and Testing Gameplay
**Official Requirement 4 (Parent Overview):** Do the following: (a) Pick a game where the players can change the rules or objectives (examples: basketball, hearts, chess, kickball). Briefly summarize the standard rules and objectives and play through the game normally. (b) Propose changes to several rules or objectives. Predict how each change will affect gameplay. (c) Play the game with one rule or objective change, observing how the players' actions and emotional experiences are affected by the rule change. Repeat this process with two other changes. (d) Explain to your counselor how the changes affected the actions and experience of the players. Discuss the accuracy of your predictions.
**Source:** *Official Scouting America Game Design Merit Badge Pamphlet (p. 49, p. 78)* • **Group Organizer:** Concept & Examples Notes
> **Guide to Safe Scouting Warning:** Apply BSA Digital Safety and Cyber Chip guidelines during Game Design Requirement 4: protect personal credentials and private data while working on modifying rules and testing gameplay, verify software sources before downloading, and include a parent or counselor on all online communications.
- **Common Myth:** Changing five or six rules at the same time is the fastest way to see which modification improves a game.
- **Pamphlet Fact:** Designers test one rule or objective change at a time so they can clearly observe how that specific adjustment alters player strategy and emotion (p. 78).

**Official Pamphlet Summary:**
- The play sequence of a game answers these questions (p. 49).
- Changing the play sequence can have a big impact on the play experience (p. 49).
- Out-of-Turn Player Actions: The Gathering there are some cards that allow players to take actions when it is someone else's turn (p. 49).
- Sustaining Player Engagement: As a result, participants remain actively engaged even when waiting for their turn (p. 49).

(a) Write a vision statement for your game. Identify the medium, player format, objectives, and theme of the game. If suitable, describe the setting, story, and characters. (b) Describe the reason that someone would want to play your game. (c) Make a preliminary list of the rules of the game. Define the resources. (d) Draw the game elements.
**Source:** *Official Scouting America Game Design Merit Badge Pamphlet (p. 20, p. 22)* • **Group Organizer:** Field Sketch & Diagram Canvas

- **Common Myth:** You must finish painting high-quality artwork and building polished components before writing down your game's vision statement or rules.
- **Pamphlet Fact:** Game design begins in a notebook with a clear vision statement, preliminary rules, resource definitions, and rough sketches before any prototype is built (p. 22).

**Official Pamphlet Summary:**
- Role of Game Master: In most RPGs, one player serves as the game master (p. 20).
- This player is the moderator and primary storyteller of the game (p. 20).
- Preparing Player Choices: A GM cooks the food, sets the table, and provides different choices for entertainment (p. 20).
- The GM is not in charge of the players but is in the game to give the players a compelling experience (p. 20).

### Req 6: Building and Playtesting Your Prototype
**Official Requirement 6 (Parent Overview):** Do the following: Note: You must have your counselor's approval of your concept before you begin creating the prototype. (a) Prototype your game from requirement 5. If applicable, demonstrate to your counselor that you have addressed player safety through the rules and equipment. Record your work in your game design notebook. (b) Test your prototype with as many other people as you need to meet the player format. Compare the play experience to your descriptions from requirement 5(b). Correct unclear rules, holes in the rules, dead ends, and obvious rule exploits. Change at least one rule, mechanic, or objective from your first version of the game, and describe why you are making the change. Play the game again. Record in your game design notebook whether or not your change had the expected effect. (c) Repeat 6(b) at least two more times and record the results in your game design notebook.
**Source:** *Official Scouting America Game Design Merit Badge Pamphlet (p. 69, p. 78)* • **Group Organizer:** 2-Column Comparison Table
> **Guide to Safe Scouting Warning:** Apply BSA Digital Safety and Cyber Chip guidelines during Game Design Requirement 6: protect personal credentials and private data while working on building and playtesting your prototype, verify software sources before downloading, and include a parent or counselor on all online communications.
> **Starting Gate (Counselor Pre-Approval Required):** Counselor Pre-Approval Required - Review your Game Design Req 6 preparation with your Merit Badge Counselor before moving to subsequent tasks.
- **Common Myth:** A first prototype should look like a finished store-bought product so playtesters take the game seriously.
- **Pamphlet Fact:** Early prototypes should be built quickly and cheaply with index cards, coins, or simple code because rules and components will change repeatedly during testing (p. 69).

**Official Pamphlet Summary:**
- Transitioning to Playtesting: Once a game prototype has been developed, testing can begin (p. 69).
- Gathering Playtester Feedback: Testing is the process of playing a game and providing feedback to the designer or development team (p. 69).
- Testing will continue throughout the game development cycle, and the purpose and design of the tests will change (p. 69).
- The game designer is acrucial participant in the first stage of testing (p. 69).

### Req 7: Blind Playtesting and Written Instructions
**Official Requirement 7 (Parent Overview):** Blind test your game. Do the following: (a) Write an instruction sheet that includes all of the information needed to play the game. Clearly describe how to set up the game, play the game, and end the game. List the game objectives. (b) Share your prototype from requirement 6 with a group of players that has not played it or witnessed a previous playtest. Provide them with your instruction sheet(s) and any physical components. Watch them play the game, but do not provide them with instruction. Record their feedback in your game design notebook. (c) Share your game design notebook with your counselor. Discuss the player reactions to your project and what you learned about the game design process. Based on your testing, determine what you like most about your game and suggest one or more changes.
**Source:** *Official Scouting America Game Design Merit Badge Pamphlet (p. 82, p. 84)* • **Group Organizer:** Activity & Observation Log
> **Guide to Safe Scouting Warning:** Apply BSA Digital Safety and Cyber Chip guidelines during Game Design Requirement 7: protect personal credentials and private data while working on blind playtesting and written instructions, verify software sources before downloading, and include a parent or counselor on all online communications.
- **Common Myth:** During a blind playtest, the designer should immediately step in and explain a rule whenever players look confused.
- **Pamphlet Fact:** In a blind test, you must hand players the prototype and written instructions without coaching them so you can see whether the rulebook stands on its own (p. 82).

**Official Pamphlet Summary:**
- Reading Tester Body Language: In addition to tracking game actions, watch body language to help you understand the players' emotional state during the test (p. 82).
- Blind testers should be similar to the people you imagine will be playing your game when it is finished (p. 82).
- Testing with Your Troop: Consider asking other Scouts or Scout leaders to test your game during a troop meeting or campout (p. 82).
- Otherwise, a family member and classmates or friends could be testers (p. 82).

### Req 8: Game Industry Careers and Education
**Official Requirement 8 (Parent Overview):** Do ONE of the following: (a) With your parent or guardian's permission and your counselor's approval, visit with a professional in the game development industry and ask them about their job and how it fits into the overall development process. (b) Meet with a professional in game development education and discuss the skills they emphasize in the classroom.
**Source:** *Official Scouting America Game Design Merit Badge Pamphlet (p. 23, p. 90)* • **Group Organizer:** Concept & Examples Notes
> **Guide to Safe Scouting Warning:** Apply BSA Digital Safety and Cyber Chip guidelines during Game Design Requirement 8: protect personal credentials and private data while working on game industry careers and education, verify software sources before downloading, and include a parent or counselor on all online communications.
> **Starting Gate (Counselor Pre-Approval Required):** Counselor Pre-Approval Required - Review your Game Design Req 8 preparation with your Merit Badge Counselor before moving to subsequent tasks.
- **Common Myth:** A professional game designer spends most of the workday playing finished video games for fun.
- **Pamphlet Fact:** Professional game development requires collaborative problem-solving across programming, art, audio, project management, quality assurance, and iterative rules design (p. 90).

**Official Pamphlet Summary:**
- Applying Design Beyond Studios: Work within the game industry is not a career everyone can pursue no matter how hard they try, but that does not mean game design cannot be incorporated (p. 23).
- Transferable Rulemaking Skills: Coming up with a fun, exciting, and balanced ruleset is a good skill to have in almost any career (p. 23).
- Teachers can employ game elements in the classroom to make learning a more engaging, participatory experience (p. 23).
- You can also use the psychology of rewarding tasks to get yourself to do things you would not normally want (p. 23).
