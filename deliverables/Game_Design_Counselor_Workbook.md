### Req 1: Game Mediums and Play Value
**Official Requirement 1 (Parent Overview):** Do the following: (a) Analyze four games you have played, each from a different medium. Identify the medium, player format, objectives, rules, resources, and theme (if relevant). Discuss with your counselor the play experience, what you enjoy in each game, and what you dislike. Make a chart to compare and contrast the games. (b) Describe four types of play value and provide an example of a game built around each concept. Discuss with your counselor other reasons people play games.
**Source:** *Official Scouting America Game Design Merit Badge Pamphlet (p. 38, p. 45)* • **Group Organizer:** 2-Column Comparison Table
> **Guide to Safe Scouting Warning:** When analyzing outdoor physical games, establish clear playing boundaries free of trip hazards, obstacles, and vehicular traffic.
- **Common Myth:** Beginners often believe digital video games share identical operational design fundamentals with physical board games.
- **Pamphlet Fact:** Physical and digital mediums demand distinct approaches to information hiding, physics resolution, and rule enforcement.

**Official Pamphlet Summary:**
- Medium Distinction: Tabletop, electronic, card, and live outdoor games each provide unique mechanical constraints.
- Resource Accounting: Games limit currency, territory, health, and turn actions to establish strategic tension.
- Player Dynamics: Competitive formats create direct opposition while cooperative formats unite players against mechanics.
- Play Motivations: Designers target diverse engagement patterns including social bonding, mastery, immersion, and discovery.

### Req 4: Rule Modification and Gameplay Testing
**Official Requirement 4 (Parent Overview):** Do the following: (a) Pick a game where the players can change the rules or objectives (examples: basketball, hearts, chess, kickball). Briefly summarize the standard rules and objectives and play through the game normally. (b) Propose changes to several rules or objectives. Predict how each change will affect gameplay. (c) Play the game with one rule or objective change, observing how the players' actions and emotional experiences are affected by the rule change. Repeat this process with two other changes. (d) Explain to your counselor how the changes affected the actions and experience of the players. Discuss the accuracy of your predictions.
**Source:** *Official Scouting America Game Design Merit Badge Pamphlet (p. 49, p. 78)* • **Group Organizer:** Concept & Examples Notes
> **Guide to Safe Scouting Warning:** Ensure participants do not engage in overly aggressive physical contact when modifying rules in athletic or field games.
- **Common Myth:** Doubling resource generation or speed always makes a game twice as exciting for participants.
- **Pamphlet Fact:** Excessive resource abundance eliminates meaningful trade-offs, reducing strategic tension and making victory feel unearned.

**Official Pamphlet Summary:**
- Playtesting baseline rules establishes standard pacing, resource balance, and typical player interactions.
- Hypothesis Formulation: Predicting mechanical outcomes requires analyzing player incentives under altered game constraints.
- Testing one rule change at a time isolates direct behavioral causation reliably.
- Outcome Verification: Comparing actual play results against initial predictions reveals subtle emergent player behaviors.

- Element Rendering: Sketching game elements clarifies scale, board layout, card iconography, and user interactions.
