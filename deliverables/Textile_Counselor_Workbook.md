### Req 2: Fiber Classification and Selection Principles
**Official Requirement 2 (Parent Overview):** Do the following: (a) Get swatches of two natural fiber fabrics (100% cotton, linen, wool, or silk; no blends). Get swatches of two synthetic fiber fabrics (nylon, polyester, acrylic, olefin, or spandex). Get a sample of one cellulosic fabric (rayon, acetate, or lyocell). (b) Give the origin, major characteristics, and general content of each type of fiber obtained for 2(a). Explain the difference between a cellulosic manufactured fiber and a synthetic manufactured fiber. (c) Describe the main steps in making raw fiber into yarn, and yarn into fabric. (d) Assume you will soon buy a new garment or other textile item. Tell your counselor what fiber or blend of fibers you want the item to be, and give reasons for your choice.
**Source:** *Official Scouting America Textile Merit Badge Pamphlet (p. 27, p. 62)* • **Group Organizer:** 2-Column Comparison Table
> **Guide to Safe Scouting Warning:** Use shears carefully with steady downward pressure when cutting stiff synthetic canvas swatches.
- **Common Myth:** Scouts frequently think rayon is a fully synthetic petrochemical plastic like nylon or polyester.
- **Pamphlet Fact:** Rayon is a manufactured cellulosic fiber processed from natural wood pulp or plant polymers.

**Official Pamphlet Summary:**
- Natural Fiber Origin: Plant crops and animal fleeces yield staple fibers without synthetic chemical synthesis.
- Polymer Extrusion Process: Synthetic petrochemical solutions pass through microscopic spinneret orifices to form continuous filaments.
- Regenerated Wood Pulp: Solubilized plant cellulose precipitates into liquid coagulation baths to produce rayon fibers.
- Mechanical Spinning Progression: Carded slivers undergo drafting and twisting operations to assemble strong continuous yarns.

### Req 3: Textile Construction and Processing Projects
**Official Requirement 3 (Parent Overview):** Do TWO of the following: (a) Visit a textile plant, textile products manufacturer, or textile school or college. Report on what you saw and learned. (b) Weave a belt, headband, placemat, or wall hanging using a simple loom that you have made yourself. (c) With a magnifying glass, examine a woven fabric, a nonwoven fabric, and a knitted fabric. Sketch what you see. Explain how the three constructions are different. (d) Make a piece of felt. (e) Make two natural dyes and use them to dye a garment or a piece of fabric. (f) Waterproof a fabric. (g) Demonstrate how to identify fibers, using microscope identification or the breaking test.
**Source:** *Official Scouting America Textile Merit Badge Pamphlet (p. 27, p. 58)* • **Group Organizer:** Field Sketch & Diagram Canvas
> **Guide to Safe Scouting Warning:** Conduct all dye boiling and fiber burn tests in well-ventilated areas using heat-resistant gloves.
- **Common Myth:** Scouts often think nonwoven felt is produced by interlocking perpendicular yarns like denim.
- **Pamphlet Fact:** Felt is a nonwoven sheet assembled by entangling unspun fibers through moisture, heat, and friction.

**Official Pamphlet Summary:**
- Longitudinal warp yarns must maintain uniform tautness on loom frames during weaving.
- Wool Felting Action: Heat, mechanical agitation, and warm soapy water cause wool scales to interlock permanently.
- Natural Mordant Binding: Mineral salts or tannins chemically bind botanical dye extracts onto receptive fabric fibers.
- Wax Waterproofing Barrier: Paraffin and beeswax coatings melt into woven yarn gaps to repel standing water.

### Req 4: Textile Terminology and Machinery Vocabulary
**Official Requirement 4:** Explain the meaning of 10 of the following terms: warp, harness, heddle, shed, aramid, spandex, sliver, yarn, spindle, distaff, loom, cellulose, sericulture, extrusion, carbon fibers, spinneret, staple, worsted, nonwoven, and greige goods.
**Source:** *Official Scouting America Textile Merit Badge Pamphlet (p. 27, p. 30)* • **Organizer (Concept & Examples Notes):** Req 4 Structured Technical Study & Explanation Matrix (Core Textile Terminology and Weaves)
> **Guide to Safe Scouting Warning:** Stay clear of moving loom heddles and rotating drive spindles during mechanized operation.
- **Common Myth:** Scouts often confuse warp and weft yarns, assuming warp runs horizontally across width.
- **Pamphlet Fact:** Warp yarns run lengthwise under tension, while weft yarns travel crosswise through the shed.
