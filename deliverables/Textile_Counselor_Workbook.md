### Req 2: Fiber Classifications and Fabric Production
**Official Requirement 2 (Parent Overview):** Do the following: (a) Get swatches of two natural fiber fabrics (100% cotton, linen, wool, or silk; no blends). Get swatches of two synthetic fiber fabrics (nylon, polyester, acrylic, olefin, or spandex). Get a sample of one cellulosic fabric (rayon, acetate, or lyocell). (b) Give the origin, major characteristics, and general content of each type of fiber obtained for 2(a). Explain the difference between a cellulosic manufactured fiber and a synthetic manufactured fiber. (c) Describe the main steps in making raw fiber into yarn, and yarn into fabric. (d) Assume you will soon buy a new garment or other textile item. Tell your counselor what fiber or blend of fibers you want the item to be, and give reasons for your choice.
**Source:** *Official Scouting America Textile Merit Badge Pamphlet (p. 27, p. 62)* • **Group Organizer:** 2-Column Comparison Table
> **Guide to Safe Scouting Warning:** Maintain a well-lit, organized craft table for Textile Requirement 2: store needles, pins, shears, and rotary cutters in closed cases immediately after working on fiber classifications and fabric production, cut away from your body, and unplug pressing irons when unattended.
- **Common Myth:** Cellulosic fibers like rayon, acetate, and lyocell are petroleum-based synthetics just like polyester and nylon.
- **Pamphlet Fact:** Cellulosic fibers are manufactured from regenerated natural plant cellulose such as wood pulp, whereas true synthetic fibers are synthesized from petrochemicals (p. 27).

**Official Pamphlet Summary:**
- Aramid fibers are increasingly being used as replacements for steel, fiberglass, asbestos, and aluminum (p. 27).
- Skis with aramid-fiber cores last longer and perform better (p. 27).
- Racing drivers wear aramid suits that help prevent them from burning to death in fiery, high-speed crashes (p. 27).
- Carbonized Precursor Fiber Production: Carbon fibers are extremely strong, stiff, lightweight fibers of nearly pure carbon made by "baking" or "charring" rayon, pitch, or acrylic fibers (p. 27).

### Req 3: Hands-On Weaving, Dyeing, and Testing
**Official Requirement 3 (Parent Overview):** Do TWO of the following: (a) Visit a textile plant, textile products manufacturer, or textile school or college. Report on what you saw and learned. (b) Weave a belt, headband, placemat, or wall hanging using a simple loom that you have made yourself. (c) With a magnifying glass, examine a woven fabric, a nonwoven fabric, and a knitted fabric. Sketch what you see. Explain how the three constructions are different. (d) Make a piece of felt. (e) Make two natural dyes and use them to dye a garment or a piece of fabric. (f) Waterproof a fabric. (g) Demonstrate how to identify fibers, using microscope identification or the breaking test.
**Source:** *Official Scouting America Textile Merit Badge Pamphlet (p. 27, p. 58)* • **Group Organizer:** Field Sketch & Diagram Canvas
> **Guide to Safe Scouting Warning:** Maintain a well-lit, organized craft table for Textile Requirement 3: store needles, pins, shears, and rotary cutters in closed cases immediately after working on hands-on weaving, dyeing, and testing, cut away from your body, and unplug pressing irons when unattended.
- **Common Myth:** Under a microscope, natural cotton and wool fibers look like smooth glass rods, while synthetic fibers look rough and scaly.
- **Pamphlet Fact:** Natural fibers have rough scales or a twisted ribbon appearance that helps them grip together in yarn, whereas manufactured fibers are naturally smooth unless texturized (p. 27).

**Official Pamphlet Summary:**
- You will notice that the natural fibers have scales or a twisted or kinky construction (p. 27).
- Cotton fiber, for instance, looks like a twisted ribbon under a microscope (p. 27).
- The rough texture of natural fibers helps the fibers stay together when they are twisted into yarns (p. 27).
- Engineered Crimp in Synthetics: Manufactured fibers are smooth or have been given lengthwise grooves and specially treated to be crimped or twisted (p. 27).

#### Req 3a: Textile Facility or School Visit
**Official Requirement 3a:** Visit a textile plant, textile products manufacturer, or textile school or college. Report on what you saw and learned.
**Source:** *Official Scouting America Textile Merit Badge Pamphlet (p. 27, p. 58)* • **Organizer (Concept & Examples Notes):** Req 3a Structured Technical Study & Explanation Matrix (Textile Plant Textile Products Manufacturer)
- **Common Myth:** Woven and knitted fabrics have the exact same grid pattern when examined under a magnifying glass.
- **Pamphlet Fact:** Woven fabrics interlace perpendicular warp and filling yarns at right angles, whereas knitted fabrics are formed from interlocking loops of yarn (p. 58).
